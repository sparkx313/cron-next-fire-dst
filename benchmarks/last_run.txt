Interpretation: over 40 years a monthly job on the 29th of February gets 480
  month slots but matches 10 days, so 470 of them are silently skipped - the
  worst wait is 48 months between firings, not 12. Day 31 in every month keeps
  280 of 480 slots (200 skips, the four short months plus February), and '0 0
  30 * *' has no long gap at all because every month except February has a
  30th. The naive reading of '0 0 30 2 *' is a job that runs on February 30th;
  the exhaustive day scan finds 0 matching days in 40 years, and the fast path
  agrees by refusing to answer: next_fire raises CronSearchLimitError after 8
  years instead of inventing a date.

5. parse and next_fire cost, with measured noise floor (timing)
==============================================================================
work                                 n       best ms   floor ms  per op us
parse full 5-field expression        20000   279.879   8.254     13.99
next_fire dense */7 3 * * *          20000   86.639    5.277     4.33
next_fire daily 0 0 * * *            20000   967.719   84.539    48.39
next_fire weekly 0 0 * * 1           10000   50.249    1.643     5.02
next_fire leap 0 0 29 2 *            3000    210.888   6.249     70.30
next_fire */20 * * * * tz=None       2000    24.437    0.170     12.22
next_fire */20 * * * * tz=New_York   2000    111.870   33.339    55.93

Interpretation: each row's floor column is its own tie band: the same-batch
  spreads run from 0.7% to 29.8% of a row's best pass on this loaded machine,
  so numbers inside one row's band are a tie and no ranking below leans on
  them. Normalised to one call the ordering is dense 4.33 us, weekly 5.02 us,
  daily 48.39 us, leap 70.30 us: cost follows how many hours and minutes the
  carry walk visits, not how often the job fires. The leap-day query is 16.2x
  dense, outside both floors (3.0% and 6.1%), because a sparse expression
  carries the search across whole months and years instead of testing minutes,
  while weekly is only 1.16x dense - outside both rows' own floors, so it is
  real. The daily row is the slowest of the three non-sparse ones at 11.2x
  dense, because reaching the next midnight walks the hour list once and the
  minute list once.

Interpretation: the DST check is measurable but small: the same '*/20 * * * *'
  query starting 20 minutes before a spring-forward gap costs 55.93 us with a
  zone against 12.22 us with tz=None, a 4.58x ratio, because minute_status
  only runs on the candidate minutes that survive field matching. Parsing is
  the surprise: 13.99 us per expression is 3.2x one dense next_fire call, so
  re-parsing on every tick would dominate the arithmetic - build the
  CronSchedule once and reuse it.
